December 6–7, 2007: The Victim Letters, the Defense Papers, and the Missing Decision Record

By December 7, the U.S. Attorney’s Office had drafted victim-notification letters, a deadline for sending them had been set, and an FBI case agent was warning that many victims had never received earlier notices. Then multiple defense submissions landed, AUSA Marie Villafaña asked for permission to send the letters, and Jeffrey Sloman replied two minutes 23 seconds later: “Hold the letter.” OPR later could not identify with certainty the person Sloman said he had spoken to.

1. November 30: a deadline existed

OPR reports that after defense counsel objected to the proposed victim-notification letter, U.S. Attorney Alexander Acosta told Kenneth Starr that prosecutors had been directed not to issue the letters until Friday, December 7 at 5 p.m. The stated purpose was to give the defense time to review its options with Epstein.

That matters because December 7 did not begin as an open-ended hold. The surviving record contains a specific date and time after which the office had said notification could proceed.

2. December 5–6: the dispute intensifies

OPR records that on December 5 Starr and Jay Lefkowitz wrote Acosta, with copies to Sloman and Assistant Attorney General Alice Fisher, reaffirming the NPA while continuing to contest parts of the government’s position.

On December 6, according to OPR’s footnote reconstruction, the FBI lead case agent told Villafaña that she was “still holding many of the original V/VW letters addressed to victims from the USAO.” The next day’s OPR record quotes the agent objecting to defense involvement in the victim-notification process and stressing the federal obligation to notify victims.

Boundary. The case agent’s concern establishes an internal warning about unnotified victims. It does not itself establish why management later stopped the December 7 mailing.

3. December 7: three tracks collide

OPR describes three materially different events converging that day:

  • The letters were ready. Villafaña prepared letters for multiple victims containing the federal resolution, anticipated state plea, victim participation options, and monetary-damages information.
  • The defense asked for another pause. Lilly Ann Sanchez wrote Sloman that Epstein’s plea hearing was set for January 4, 2008 and requested that the office “hold off” on sending victim-notification letters until the contents could be discussed.
  • The defense also submitted two ethics opinions. Starr and Lefkowitz sent Acosta lengthy “independent ethics opinions” supporting defense arguments against the federal investigation and against the NPA’s use of 18 U.S.C. § 2255.

The timing establishes a crowded decision environment. It does not establish which of those inputs caused the operational stop.

5. The missing decision record

OPR later interviewed Sloman about the hold. He said he wanted to push the victim letter out, but that his instruction was “the product of me speaking to somebody,” while also saying he could not be definitive about who that person was.

That is the narrow unresolved point. The available public record does not identify the person with certainty. It does not preserve the content of the decisive conversation. And it does not provide a contemporaneous memorandum explaining why the office’s previously stated December 7 notification deadline was replaced by the hold.

A missing explanation is not proof of a hidden order. It is an evidentiary gap with defined boundaries.

7. The letters did not simply disappear from the story

OPR says Villafaña contacted counsel for the victim later known as Jane Doe #2 on December 10 and said she was preparing victim-notification letters. A December 13 Villafaña letter to Lefkowitz later said that three victims had received general notice shortly after the NPA was signed and that further notifications stopped after defense objections. On December 19, the office told defense counsel it would notify victims of the federal resolution as required by law while leaving state-proceeding notice to state authorities.

The sequence therefore shows repeated attempts to move notification forward, repeated defense objections, and repeated management pauses—not a single isolated email.

8. OPR’s conclusion is a necessary counterweight

DOJ OPR did not find professional misconduct by Acosta or the other prosecutors and did not find evidence that the process was driven by corruption or another impermissible motive. OPR did, however, conclude that victims were not treated with the forthrightness and sensitivity expected by the Department and that Acosta exercised poor judgment in resolving the federal investigation through the NPA.

GAH preserves both parts. The December 7 decision gap is real. A corrupt explanation for that gap is not established by the surviving record.

9. The open receipts

  • Identity of Sloman’s “somebody”: unresolved in OPR’s interview record.
  • Content of the decisive conversation: not preserved in the public record reviewed here.
  • Complete December 7 communications: additional phone logs, internal notes, or message traffic could narrow the gap if recovered.
  • Defense-submission chronology: exact receipt/read times for the Sanchez letter and two ethics opinions could help reconstruct what Sloman had before him when the hold was sent.
