Andrew’s Search Warrants Were Quashed in July. The Separate 2020 DOJ Judicial-Review Trail.

The legal result emerged publicly on October 8, but the High Court quashed the warrants on July 22. A released U.S. Department of Justice email from June 2020 records UK officials anticipating a judicial-review challenge by Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor’s lawyers in a different legal proceeding. The parallel is real; the two cases are not the same.

Grok Archive Hub Editorial Desk · October 8, 2026

Read the timelineRead the DOJ recordSource manifest

Central finding. On July 22, 2026, the High Court quashed search warrants issued in February for properties associated with Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor after their statutory basis was found incorrect. Today’s October 8 hearing put that previously decided outcome in public view. Independently, DOJ correspondence from June 18, 2020 records UK officials expecting Andrew’s lawyers to challenge a separate mutual legal assistance request by judicial review. This does not establish that anyone predicted the 2026 search-warrant ruling or that using legal process was wrongdoing.

What happened in 2026

On February 18, 2026, a Central Criminal Court judge granted warrants to search a residence on the Sandringham estate and Royal Lodge in Windsor. Andrew was arrested the following day on suspicion of misconduct in public office, following allegations he had shared sensitive material with Jeffrey Epstein while acting as the United Kingdom’s trade envoy. Andrew denies wrongdoing. He was questioned and released under investigation, with no criminal charge announced as of October 8.

During the later High Court litigation, Thames Valley Police accepted that the warrants had been issued under the wrong statutory mechanism. Mr Justice Hilliard’s October 8 account distinguished the normal section 8 process under the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 from the section 9 procedure implicated by material potentially subject to legal privilege or the special-procedure regime. The application itself contemplated searching for sensitive or confidential material.

According to the police statement reported on October 8, the High Court quashed the warrants on July 22, 2026. The finding concerns the lawfulness of the search warrants. As the judge explained, it does not automatically invalidate the overall investigation.

Police also applied in June to retain material obtained in the searches. Andrew opposes that application; further private proceedings are expected in 2027. No final ruling on retention or eventual admissibility is established by today’s judgment.

The October 8 hearing had been scheduled around police efforts to restrict access to documents filed in the judicial-review case. The judge said police notified the court on October 7 that they would no longer pursue that restriction application. The reasons and the rules that governed public access before today warrant further reporting.

The 2020 DOJ paper trail

An April 3, 2020 letter from the U.S. Department of Justice’s Office of International Affairs (DOJ No. CRM-182-73090, EFTA00022062–EFTA00022083) sought UK assistance in interviewing Andrew. It concerned two U.S. investigations: the ongoing Epstein investigation and a separate Peter Nygard investigation. In the request Andrew was described as a witness, not a current target, and the DOJ said it had not then collected evidence that he committed a crime under U.S. law. The DOJ requested a voluntary interview first, with compelled testimony contemplated if he declined.

The sharper record is the June 18, 2020 correspondence preserved as EFTA00088020–EFTA00088023. In the email chain, U.S. officials relay UK concerns that the Home Office’s approval of the MLA request could prompt judicial-review litigation. They specifically anticipated that Andrew’s solicitors, Blackfords, would argue the Home Office had not followed internal guidance requiring serious pursuit of voluntary cooperation before formal legal assistance.

The same chain records disagreement over that proposed sequence. The U.S. side maintained that the mutual legal assistance treaty did not impose a categorical requirement to exhaust every informal avenue first. UK officials were described as trying to close off a litigation argument they expected to be raised. That is a documented procedural disagreement, not evidence of a secret plan.

The decisive distinction: the 2020 anticipated challenge involved Home Office approval of a U.S. witness-interview request. The 2026 successful judicial review involved search warrants for a new UK police investigation. The archival record does not prove that Blackfords actually filed the anticipated 2020 challenge, and does not link the two cases as one continuous proceeding.

The open questions

Why did July 22 only become publicly known on October 8? The gap is documented, but the reasons remain unclear. A complete court file and the relevant public-access orders would be needed before attributing intent.

What exactly was seized, and what may police retain? The evidentiary fate of material from the searches is the subject of separate pending proceedings. The content and ultimate use of that material cannot be assumed.

Was the 2020 anticipated judicial review ever filed? The DOJ email records a prediction by officials, not proof of a completed filing. The relevant 2020 Home Office and court dockets are an open research target.

Bottom line

The 2026 warrant ruling is a significant judicial check on search powers. The 2020 emails supply a narrower, independently documented historical parallel: officials knew that formal U.S. requests involving Andrew could face judicial-review litigation. The responsible conclusion is about the paper trail and procedural choices, not about guilt, obstruction, or a coordinated six-year plan.
