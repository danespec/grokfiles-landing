FBI Vault Part 13: what the March redaction instructions and July statement emails actually show

The FBI has published a contemporaneous 2025 redaction-workflow chronology and an FBI–DOJ email chain. Those records illuminate who directed work and when. They do not, by themselves, establish that the records are part of the certified administrative record sought in Phang v. Blanche.

The central distinction: The FBI records concern work performed in March and July 2025. The Epstein Files Transparency Act was enacted on November 19, 2025, and the litigation addresses the later disclosure regime. A thematic overlap is not proven record identity, causation, or an unlawful withholding.

Source chain: what was released

The FBI Vault lists Records regarding the review of the Investigative Holdings related to Jeffrey Epstein, Part 13. The FBI's recent additions index dates Part 13 to October 8, 2026. Its 32-page original PDF includes an “Epstein Overview” chronology and emails bearing FBI FOIA page markings. The 2026 publication date is not the date the events occurred.

March 22, 2025: two distinct redaction instructions

On PDF page 2, FBI(25-cv-2791)-5, the chronology reports victim-name-only processing across six cases, totaling 116,792 pages. Footnote 2 describes a list totaling 1,115 names, including victims and their family members. This is not described as a clientele list, and GAH will not relabel it.

The same dated entry identifies new direction received by email from DOJ's Office of the Deputy Attorney General, limited to the 50D (SDNY) and 31E (SDFL) cases. It describes expanding beyond victim names to witnesses, law-enforcement personnel, AUSAs, and grand-jury subfiles. Footnote 3 specifies that the DOJ scoping guidance excluded grand-jury subfiles from that redaction project, while grand-jury content encountered elsewhere in investigative material was to be redacted. The two statements are not contradictory: one concerns project inclusion; the other, treatment of encountered content.

On March 23, the chronology says FBI-generated training materials based on DOJ scope guidance were furnished to participating FBI personnel. On March 25, it lists additional redaction-project inventories, counts, and samples provided to DOJ. These establish a documentary trail to seek; the underlying instruction email, version-controlled protocol, and full delivery receipts must be compared before making a compliance finding.

July 3, 2025: statement edits and video hosting

PDF pages 9–12, FBI(25-cv-2791)-273 through -276, show exchanges among FBI officials Kashyap Patel and Daniel Bongino, Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche, and White House communications personnel. One July 3 morning email says it is circulating edits requested by Blanche. Blanche's 10:18 a.m. email seeks a change to the second paragraph of a joint statement and asks which agency should host related video. The actual proposed sentence is withheld in the released copy.

Later that afternoon, Blanche raises a technical problem transferring video from FBI to DOJ hosting. Other messages discuss final document links, possible FBI/DOJ joint letterhead, and distribution to the White House communications team. The available correspondence establishes consultation and logistical coordination—not the content of the redacted edits, the final video's provenance, or a covert motive.

How it intersects with Phang v. Blanche—and where the join stops

In Phang v. Blanche, No. 1:26-cv-01417 (D.D.C.), Judge Emmet G. Sullivan's Phang, ECF 59
October 16 DOJ Notice of Compliance reporting 10:55 a.m. deliverySeptember 16, 2026 order, ECF 42, directed DOJ to file a certified list of the contents of its administrative record by October 1 and produce the record to the plaintiff by October 15. A separate September 16 order, ECF 43, addressed disputed redactions, underlying FBI handwritten interview notes, and foreign-language materials. In considering handwritten notes, the court rejected the government's position that notes were necessarily duplicative of typed FD-302 reports.

The Epstein Files Transparency Act, sections 2(b) and 2(c), bars redaction solely for embarrassment or political sensitivity, permits specified protections including victim privacy and certain other statutory categories, and requires written redaction justifications. The March 2025 project predates that law; later decisions must be tested against the law and the 2026 records, not retroactively labeled illegal from chronology alone.

October 17 source update — ECF 59: The signed October 9 ECF 57 order directed redacted handwritten FBI notes underlying FD-302s EFTA01245620, EFTA02858481 and EFTA02858491 to be submitted to Judge Sullivan for in-camera review by noon Eastern October 16. The government then filed its October 16 ECF 59 Notice of Compliance, reporting that it hand-delivered a binder containing the notes marked for redaction to the judge's chambers at 10:55 a.m. Eastern — before the deadline. Government-reported timely submission is now documented. ECF 59 is not independent judicial verification of the binder's contents, a ruling approving the redactions, or an order to release the notes publicly. The separate administrative-record deadlines and ECF 51 postponement question remain independently unresolved in this source review. Read GAH's ECF 59 source report.

Three independent questions remain open: whether the ECF 42 certified administrative-record list was filed with the court October 1, whether the full record was delivered to the plaintiff October 15, and whether a signed ruling granted the government's October 1 ECF 51 motion to postpone the deadlines. Filing a motion is not a stay; the ECF 52 Rule 12(c) motion is not a final judgment; plaintiff's ECF 55 opposition is advocacy, not an order. Without a complete authenticated docket, the FBI Vault chronology cannot be declared included in, or excluded from, the administrative record.
