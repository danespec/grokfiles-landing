Nine SARs, $1.28 Billion, and the Reporting Gap in Epstein’s JPMorgan Accounts

Seven JPMorgan suspicious-activity reports filed between 2002 and 2016 flagged $4.316 million. Two more reports filed in August and September 2019 covered about $1.283 billion in suspicious activity dating back years. The public record makes the timing itself an evidence question.

Grok Archive Hub Editorial Desk · October 7, 2026

Open the nine-SAR table Banking records

Bottom line. A November 2025 Senate Finance Committee staff memorandum reproduces a nine-report chronology from unsealed JPMorgan records. Seven reports filed before 2019 totaled $4,316,424. Two reports filed after Epstein’s July 2019 federal arrest totaled $1,282,799,188 and reached back to activity beginning in 2003. The reports identify suspicious activity; they are not findings that every transaction was criminal.

The first seven reports total $4,316,424. The final two total $1,282,799,188. Senate staff emphasized the scale difference because the 2019 reports swept in thousands of wires and years of earlier activity.

2. The real issue is timing

A SAR is a regulatory report of suspicious activity. It is not a criminal verdict. The strongest question in this record is therefore not whether every transfer in those filings was unlawful. It is why such a large amount of historical activity appeared in reports filed only in 2019.

The Senate memorandum identifies a five-year period from May 2003 to September 2008 in which JPMorgan filed no SAR on Epstein while, according to the same memorandum’s citation to the Amador report, Epstein withdrew more than $3.5 million in cash. Senate staff treated the gap as evidence warranting further investigation of the bank’s monitoring and reporting practices.

3. The 4,725-wire number is narrower than the nine-SAR total

New evidence brief: 4,725 Wires, ~$1.1 Billion: What the JPMorgan Number Actually Means →

A September 24, 2025 letter from Senator Ron Wyden states that Senate Finance staff reviewed Treasury files in camera and saw a 2019 JPMorgan filing describing 4,725 wire transfers totaling approximately $1.1 billion through Epstein’s JPMorgan accounts.

That number is not the sum of all nine SARs. It is a figure from one 2019 filing described by the committee. The two 2019 SARs in the later nine-report table together cover more than 5,000 suspicious wire transfers and approximately $1.283 billion.

Boundary: the Senate source describes the 4,725 wires as activity involving Epstein and associates through Epstein’s JPMorgan accounts. It does not support viral claims that the 4,725 transfers were all payments from any one outside person to Epstein.

4. Epstein was not an invisible retail customer

The same Senate memorandum says unsealed JPMorgan records described Epstein as one of the private bank’s largest clients and as part of an elite group referred to internally as the “Wall of Cash.” A 2003 due-diligence report said his accounts generated one of the largest annual revenue flows among private-bank clients. A 2009 private-bank list showed at least $142 million in balances.

In a May 2012 review, the memorandum says Epstein ranked eighth among a team’s “Top 20 clients” by revenue, with more than $1.6 million in annual revenue. The memorandum, citing the Amador report, says JPMorgan appears to have earned more than $8.1 million in fees from Epstein between 2009 and 2014.

5. Internal concern existed before 2019

The record does not begin with hindsight after Epstein’s federal arrest. The Senate memorandum cites internal compliance and private-bank communications about repeated cash withdrawals, client-risk reviews, and the handling of information between bankers and compliance personnel.

The staff memorandum also says that, after JPMorgan ended Epstein’s direct client relationship in 2013, senior private-bank personnel discussed continuing to work with him on projects for third-party clients, including Leon Black. Senate staff argues that the correspondence may help explain why the relationship did not end cleanly when the bank exited Epstein as a customer.

6. What JPMorgan says

The Senate memorandum quotes an October 10, 2025 JPMorgan response saying that, with the exception of former executive Jes Staley, the firm’s current and former executives were respected professionals who acted with integrity and would not have allowed Epstein to remain a client had they known of ongoing crimes.

Senate Finance staff disputes that explanation and calls for further investigation. GAH treats those positions as competing claims in the public record rather than replacing either with an unsupported conclusion.

7. What the nine-SAR record directly establishes

  • Verified chronology: nine JPMorgan SARs tied to Epstein’s accounts are listed in the Senate memorandum from 2002 through 2019.
  • Verified scale difference: seven pre-2019 reports total $4.316 million; two 2019 reports total about $1.283 billion.
  • Verified historical reach: both 2019 filings include activity dating back to 2003.
  • Verified reporting gap identified by Senate staff: the memorandum describes multi-year periods without a SAR despite large cash withdrawals.
  • Verified client significance: cited JPMorgan records describe Epstein as a major private-bank client and substantial revenue source.

8. What it does not establish

  • A SAR does not establish that every transaction listed in it was criminal.
  • The public record does not, by itself, establish the reason each earlier transaction was not contemporaneously reported.
  • The existence of senior-client contact does not by itself establish knowledge of specific criminal conduct.
  • The 4,725-wire figure does not identify 4,725 transfers from one specific outside person.
  • Senate staff allegations of legal or compliance violations remain allegations unless established through the relevant legal or regulatory process.

9. The open receipts

  • Complete Treasury SAR file: the public still does not have the full Treasury collection reviewed by Senate Finance investigators.
  • Underlying 2019 SAR narratives: the public chronology is clear, but the complete unredacted transaction-level narratives remain necessary for full reconstruction.
  • Bank decision chain: which specific people made which monitoring/reporting decisions for each gap remains a document-level question.
  • Cross-bank joins: Deutsche Bank, Bank of America, BNY Mellon, correspondent banks, and other institutions must be reconstructed separately before activity can be joined responsibly.

10. Why EFTA02810827 matters next

The nine-SAR table is the headline chronology. The deeper transaction architecture sits partly in the June 16, 2023 forensic-accounting report by Jorge Amador, released in the Epstein corpus as EFTA02810827. The Senate memorandum repeatedly relies on that report for cash-withdrawal, fee, client-revenue, and transaction-analysis findings.

GAH has now published that document-level breakdown: EFTA02810827: What Jorge Amador’s Forensic Report Actually Found. It separates the report’s transaction findings from the expert-opinion layer and later Senate use of the same record.
