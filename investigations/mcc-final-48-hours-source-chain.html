The Final 48 Hours at MCC: What the Video Shows—and What It Doesn’t

The official record contains both severe custodial failures and a narrower video finding than many retellings suggest. One surviving camera recorded the SHU common area and approaches to Epstein’s tier, but not his cell door. DOJ OIG found no one entering that tier from the common area between about 10:40 p.m. and 6:30 a.m. At the same time, required rounds and counts were not performed, records were falsified, Epstein was left without the directed cellmate, and most relevant camera feeds were not being recorded.

Grok Archive Hub Editorial Desk · October 8, 2026

Open the source timelineOpen DOJ OIG 23-085

Core finding. The OIG record supports two things at once: MCC staff failures were extensive and documented; the OIG nevertheless found no evidence contradicting the FBI’s determination that there was no criminality in how Epstein died. The limited-video problem is real, but “limited video” is not the same as “no video.”

1. August 8: MCC discovers that much of its video was not recording

DOJ OIG found that MCC New York’s digital video recorder system had malfunctioned on July 29. Cameras continued to provide live feeds, but recordings were created for only about half of the cameras. Personnel discovered the problem on August 8, two days before Epstein was found dead, and the system was not repaired until afterward.

That is the first important distinction: the camera system had not gone completely dark. The failure produced a partial recording record.

2. August 9: the cellmate leaves and is not replaced

After the July 23 incident that put Epstein on suicide watch, MCC’s Psychology Department had directed that he be housed with an appropriate cellmate. On August 9, his cellmate was transferred out of MCC. OIG found that staff knew Epstein was alone but failed to ensure that another cellmate was assigned.

The OIG also found that a supervisor allowed Epstein to make an unmonitored telephone call that evening. That was one of several departures from policy identified in the report.

3. Required counts and rounds stop

BOP procedures required institutional counts and 30-minute rounds in the Special Housing Unit. OIG found that no required SHU counts were actually conducted after the 4:00 p.m. count on August 9 and that no 30-minute rounds were conducted after approximately 10:40 p.m.

Staff records nevertheless indicated that counts and rounds had occurred. Two officers, Tova Noel and Michael Thomas, were later federally charged with falsifying records. Their charges were ultimately dismissed after successful completion of deferred-prosecution agreements. OIG says SDNY declined prosecution for other employees it found had created false documentation.

4. 10:40 p.m. to 6:30 a.m.: the narrow video finding

Only one recorded SHU camera provided relevant overnight footage. OIG says that camera captured a large part of the SHU common area and portions of stairways leading to the individual tiers, including Epstein’s tier.

It did not show Epstein’s cell door.

OIG reviewed the surviving footage and found that between approximately 10:40 p.m. on August 9 and 6:30 a.m. on August 10, no one was seen entering Epstein’s cell tier from the SHU common area.

Boundary. The footage supports the proposition that the recorded approach to the tier showed no entry during that window. It is not a complete camera view of Epstein’s cell, and most MCC camera feeds were not being recorded because of the DVR failure.

5. The cell itself had another documented failure: excess linens

OIG found that required cell searches were not performed. Had Epstein’s cell been searched as required, the report says staff would have discovered an excessive amount of blankets, linens, and clothing. The OIG concluded that the combination of failures left him alone and unmonitored with excess linens for hours.

6. About 6:30 a.m.: discovery

At approximately 6:30 a.m. on August 10, staff discovered Epstein hanged in his locked cell. The New York City Office of Chief Medical Examiner determined the cause of death was hanging and the manner was suicide.

The medical examiner told OIG that the injuries were consistent with suicide by hanging and that there were no defensive wounds or other physical signs the examiner would expect from a struggle. OIG also cites the absence of debris under the fingernails, marks on the hands, knuckle contusions, or other body bruising indicating a fight.

8. The strongest finding is institutional failure, not a complete surveillance record

The official record is severe without embellishment: missed rounds, missed counts, false paperwork, staffing problems, failure to follow the cellmate directive, excess linens, and a known recorder malfunction that left most camera feeds without preserved video.

Those facts explain why the case continues to generate suspicion. But the correct evidentiary response is not to convert missing video into proof of an unseen actor. It is to preserve exactly what the surviving camera showed, exactly what it did not show, and exactly which institutional controls failed.

9. Open receipt slots

  • Native surviving video: preservation hashes, complete chain of custody, and the full unedited recording interval should remain independently inspectable wherever legally releasable.
  • DVR failure records: maintenance logs, vendor/service activity, error logs, and the July 29–August repair history are central to understanding why only part of the system recorded.
  • Key/control records: OIG describes tightly controlled SHU and tier access; the underlying access and key-accountability records remain important corroboration.
  • Cell-search history: BOP records did not show when Epstein’s cell had last been searched.

10. What this investigation does not claim

  • It does not claim the camera system was fully functional.
  • It does not claim the surviving camera showed Epstein’s cell door.
  • It does not infer a homicide from missing camera recordings or falsified staff records.
  • It does not excuse MCC staff failures because the OIG found no evidence of criminality in the death.
  • It does not treat the medical examiner’s conclusion as a substitute for the custodial-failure investigation.
