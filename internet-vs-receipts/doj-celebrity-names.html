Did DOJ Tell Reviewers to Hide Celebrity Names in the Epstein Files?

The 41-page March 2025 email release documents public-figure flags. See what that means—and what the missing spreadsheet leaves unresolved.

GAH Research Desk · October 19, 2026 · Reinterprets earlier source audits; no new underlying documents claimed

The short answer

The released instructions tell reviewers to flag additional prominent names for review. They do not, by themselves, document an order to automatically suppress those names.

What the records show

  • The interim DOJ Office of Information Policy release contains 41 pages of correspondence about the March 2025 review of Epstein investigative holdings.
  • A March 23 email refers to an existing list or spreadsheet of public figures and asks reviewers to flag additional names encountered.
  • The review discussions also address identifying information about victims, redactions, training and workflow decisions.

What they do not prove

  • The release does not include the referenced spreadsheet or the full approved review plan.
  • A flag for attorney review does not establish a redaction, concealment or an outcome for any specific name.
  • The presence of a person’s name in investigative material does not prove their participation in criminal conduct.

Why the distinction matters

The language of “flagging” matters. A review instruction describes a process step. To determine whether a named entry was concealed, researchers would need the source document, its before/after release versions and a corresponding redaction or withholding decision. None of that can be inferred from the flag alone.

The missing receipt

The complete review plan, actual name spreadsheet and document-by-document decisions would distinguish routine escalation from improper withholding. Until obtained, that narrower question remains open.
