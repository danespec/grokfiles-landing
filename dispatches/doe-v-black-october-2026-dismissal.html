Doe v. Black: Dismissal With Prejudice, Earlier Sanctions, and No Trial Verdict

A signed October 15, 2026 court order (ECF 485) confirms that Judge Jessica G. L. Clarke dismissed the civil action with prejudice under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 41(a)(2) after the plaintiff requested dismissal. Black did not oppose that request. The court expressly left a separate attorney-fees motion (ECF 440) for later decision. This is not a trial verdict on the underlying assault allegations, which Black denies.

Correction · October 21, 2026 · Signed order verified

This report previously marked the October dismissal order as an outstanding receipt. That gap is now closed: ECF 485, dated October 15, 2026 (signed court PDF), is publicly accessible. The judge identifies the plaintiff’s October 12 dismissal request as ECF 481, Black’s October 14 non-opposition as ECF 483, and explicitly preserves the pending attorney-fees motion, ECF 440. We have inspected ECF 485, not the complete underlying filings 481 or 483. Missing entries in public mirrors do not establish what the full current docket contains.

The short answer: what happened?

In July 2023, Jane Doe filed a civil complaint in the Southern District of New York alleging that Black sexually assaulted her in 2002, when she was a minor, after events she connected to Jeffrey Epstein. These were allegations in a pleading, not established facts. Black has repeatedly denied wrongdoing.

According to October 16 reporting, the plaintiff, who was acting without counsel, sought dismissal in a submission dated October 12. She cited health-related consequences of continuing the proceedings and maintained that the request was not a concession that her allegations were untrue. The details of a litigant’s private health are unnecessary for this report.

The signed October 15 order (ECF 485) independently confirms the court action that Black’s attorney Susan Estrich announced in a public statement dated October 16. Black’s defense rejected the plaintiff’s allegations, and counsel said there was no settlement or inducement. Those latter assertions remain party representations, not findings in ECF 485.

What the April sanctions ruling actually found

Before the voluntary-dismissal reporting, Black sought case-terminating sanctions. Judge Clarke addressed that request in a lengthy, signed April 23 Opinion and Order, ECF 388. The court made serious and specific findings concerning the conduct of the plaintiff and her former counsel:

Misrepresentations by former counsel

The court found that Jeanne Christensen made repeated false representations to the court and opposing counsel about developments in related litigation.

Destruction of a relevant social-media account

The ruling addressed instructions by counsel concerning deletion of the plaintiff’s account, with evidentiary consequences; it did not treat every allegation in the complaint as disproven.

Falsified sonogram images

The court found that portions of submitted personal journals contained falsified sonogram imagery, barring those materials for use in prosecuting the action.

Sanctions—but no termination then

The court imposed remedies including attorneys’ fees and restrictions on evidence, while finding that lesser sanctions could address the misconduct without ending the suit at that stage.

The opinion also protected the plaintiff’s ability to proceed anonymously and directed particular materials to be unsealed only with redactions. That privacy protection remains an important part of the source record.

Crucial limit: The April judge’s finding that particular evidence was falsified is an actual judicial finding. It must be reported. But the judge expressly distinguished that misconduct from the underlying assault allegation and declined to terminate the case. Saying the judge conclusively adjudicated the entire complaint as fabricated goes beyond that ruling. Black’s counsel has offered a stronger interpretation in public statements; readers should recognize it as advocacy, not quote it as the court’s holding.

What dismissal with prejudice resolves—and what it does not

Legal consequence: A dismissal with prejudice ordinarily prevents the plaintiff from refiling the same claim as a new lawsuit, apart from whatever appellate or exceptional procedural relief may be available. It ends this civil suit as a live merits proceeding.

What it is not: It is not an acquittal in a criminal case. It is not a jury verdict that the alleged assault occurred, nor a jury verdict that it did not occur. The plaintiff’s reported statement that she stood by her allegations is also not proof of their truth. The public record can contain both an adverse evidence-sanctions ruling and an unadjudicated substantive allegation.

Separate proceedings remain separate: Civil litigation, congressional inquiries, questions about Epstein-related financial transactions, and the separate SDNY referral-status record reviewed by GAH have different evidentiary standards and procedural histories. The dismissal of this complaint does not automatically answer those other questions.

Reader rule

The disposition is not a merits finding. Document the dismissal; preserve the court’s specific sanctions findings; attribute both sides’ claims; do not turn a procedural endpoint into an unsupported finding of guilt or innocence.
