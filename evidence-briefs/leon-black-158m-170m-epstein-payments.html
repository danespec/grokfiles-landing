$158 Million Became $170 Million: Reconstructing Leon Black’s Payments to Jeffrey Epstein

Two different totals now circulate for Leon Black’s payments to Jeffrey Epstein. The 2021 Dechert review commissioned by Apollo’s Conflicts Committee found $158 million in payments from 2012 through 2017. Senate Finance later said its investigation found the true total was $170 million. Those numbers come from different evidentiary stages and should not be collapsed into one another.

The clean reading

$158 million is the independently published 2021 review total. $170 million is the later Senate-investigation total.

HIGH confidence that both totals appear in official public records. The difference is not itself proof of concealment, criminality, or an improper payment purpose. It is a source discrepancy that requires transaction-level reconciliation.

1. The $158 million number

Dechert LLP’s January 2021 review was commissioned by Apollo’s Conflicts Committee to examine Black’s relationship with Epstein and any connection between Epstein and Apollo. Dechert said it reviewed more than 60,000 documents and interviewed more than 20 witnesses.

The review found that from 2012 through 2017, Epstein provided Black and the Family Office advice involving trust and estate planning, tax, philanthropy, artwork, aircraft and yacht matters, and family-office operations. It stated that payments for that work totaled $158 million.

Dechert also said it had seen no evidence that Black paid Epstein for services other than the legitimate advisory work described in the report, and no evidence that Black, the Family Office, or Apollo participated in Epstein’s criminal activity. Those are Dechert’s investigative findings, not a court judgment.

2. Dechert did not describe every idea as successful

The same review contains an important internal tension. It credits Epstein with substantive work and says Black and witnesses believed some projects created very large value. But it also says not all of Epstein’s advice was useful, that some ideas did not hold up under scrutiny, and that other lawyers, accountants, and Family Office professionals regularly vetted his work.

That tension later became central to Senate Finance’s inquiry: if other professional advisers were checking Epstein’s work and were paid much less, how was Epstein’s extraordinary compensation determined?

3. Senate Finance reopened the compensation question

In July 2023, Senate Finance said its investigation had been prompted by inconsistencies in the Dechert review and by the unusually large compensation. The committee noted that Epstein was neither a licensed tax attorney nor a certified public accountant and sought documentation showing how Black and Epstein agreed on payment amounts.

The committee also highlighted that at least some years lacked a written services agreement and questioned whether all of the compensation was properly characterized as payment for services rather than another form of transfer. Those were investigative questions, not final tax findings.

4. The total moves to $170 million

By March 2025, Senate Finance publicly stated that its investigators had found documents showing that the true total Black paid Epstein was $170 million, which the committee described as $12 million more than the amount identified by the Apollo-commissioned review.

The committee did not, in that public announcement, publish a complete transaction-by-transaction reconciliation showing every item that explains the $12 million difference. That missing reconciliation is the key receipt still needed to join the two totals cleanly.

5. The 2026 reconstruction narrows the high-dollar years

A June 4, 2026 Senate Finance letter to House Oversight states that Black paid Epstein approximately $120 million in 2013 and 2014 alone: $50 million in 2013 and $70 million in 2014. The same letter says the total reached approximately $150 million during 2013–2015.

The letter contrasts those figures with approximately $1.98 million that Black paid Paul Weiss in 2013 and 2014 for estate-planning and family-office services. That comparison is a committee argument about proportionality; it does not prove that services from the two providers were identical.

6. The USVI settlement is a separate lane

Government of the U.S. Virgin Islands financial statements report that in 2023 the government entered into a $62.5 million settlement with Black related to financial transactions and communications with Epstein. That settlement is separate from the $158 million / $170 million compensation question.

Settlement payment, advisory compensation, bank transfers, tax treatment, and allegations raised in later referrals are different evidentiary categories. GAH does not merge them simply because they involve the same people.

8. What remains open

  • The $12 million reconciliation: which exact transactions appear in the Senate total but not the Dechert total?
  • Compensation methodology: which projects generated which payment amounts, and what contemporaneous valuation record exists?
  • Tax treatment: how were individual payments characterized by Black, Epstein entities, banks, and tax authorities?
  • Bank reporting: when did financial institutions flag or report the largest payments, and what did the SAR narratives say?
  • Transaction purpose: large wires establish money movement; purpose requires separate documentary support.

Why this matters to the larger Epstein money map

The Black payments are among the largest documented revenue streams associated with Epstein after his 2008 conviction. They intersect with Southern Trust, major banks, tax and estate structures, and later government investigations. The useful task is not to substitute suspicion for proof; it is to reconcile the numbers, dates, entities, banks, and stated purposes down to the transaction level.
