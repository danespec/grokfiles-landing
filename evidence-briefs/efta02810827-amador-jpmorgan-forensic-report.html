EFTA02810827: What Jorge Amador’s Forensic Report Actually Found

The 161-page June 16, 2023 report is one of the most information-dense financial records in the Epstein corpus. It was prepared by forensic accountant Jorge Amador for the U.S. Virgin Islands litigation against JPMorgan. Its value is substantial—but its evidentiary status matters: it is an expert report prepared for one side in civil litigation, not a criminal finding, a SAR, or a judicial determination.

The report is best read as a transaction map plus an expert interpretation layer.

1. What this document is

EFTA02810827 is the filed expert report of forensic accountant Jorge Amador, dated June 16, 2023, in Government of the United States Virgin Islands v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. The report analyzes Epstein-related JPMorgan accounts, bank records, transaction data, corporate entities, and other produced material.

The report was prepared for litigation. That means the correct reading rule is: separate the underlying transaction record from the expert’s characterization of that record. A table of checks, wires, cash withdrawals, or account activity can be independently testable. A statement that the pattern was suspicious or lacked a legitimate business purpose is an expert opinion unless independently established elsewhere.

2. Amador’s conclusion is broader than a single transaction theory

In the report’s conclusion, Amador states that Epstein had a significant financial relationship with JPMorgan and that, in his opinion, the volume and complexity of the accounts were suspicious. He identifies several categories of activity he considered highly unusual, including:

  • excessive cash transactions;
  • millions of dollars in direct and indirect payments to women that he said lacked an apparent business or lawful purpose;
  • numerous foreign-currency transactions;
  • more than $5 million in payments to law firms and other litigation-related entities;
  • payments involving the C.O.U.Q. Foundation that he analyzed in the charitable-purpose context;
  • Equity-account activity, including ZBS, LLC credit-card use, that he considered inconsistent with a legitimate business purpose; and
  • significant activity through 20 shell companies that, in his analysis, lacked independent economic value beyond receiving transfers from Epstein’s personal accounts.

One additional conclusion in the filed report remains redacted. GAH does not infer the missing language.

3. The cash trail is one of the report’s strongest bridges to later Senate work

Later Senate Finance investigators repeatedly relied on Amador’s cash analysis. The committee’s November 2025 JPMorgan memorandum cites the report for more than $3.5 million in cash withdrawals between 2003 and 2013 during a period that included a five-year gap in JPMorgan SAR filings.

The same Senate memorandum reproduces a separate bank SAR describing twenty $40,000 checks—$800,000 in total—cashed from January 2007 through June 2008. That SAR is a bank compliance record, not merely Amador’s opinion, and therefore provides an independent regulatory layer for part of the cash pattern.

4. Payments to women: transaction finding versus inference

Amador’s report aggregates millions of dollars in payments to women across Epstein-related JPMorgan accounts. Later court filings reproduced parts of those expert summaries, and Senate investigators continued to cite the report as part of their financial reconstruction.

Important boundary: a payment to a woman does not by itself establish trafficking, abuse, recruitment, knowledge, or an unlawful purpose. The expert grouped transactions because of patterns, counterparties, timing, and the surrounding record. Each stronger inference requires its own evidence.

5. The Maxwell payment lane is separately significant

The Senate Finance memorandum, citing page 46 of the Amador report, states that Epstein paid at least $25 million to Ghislaine Maxwell from his JPMorgan accounts, including a one-time payment of $19 million. Senate staff described those payments as highly suspicious in light of Maxwell’s later conviction.

GAH preserves the distinction: the existence and amount of a transfer can be transaction evidence; the reason for the transfer and what it proves about knowledge or criminal conduct require separate source support.

6. Shell-company activity is a major structural finding

Amador’s conclusion says the accounts reflected significant activity through 20 shell companies that, in his analysis, had no independent economic value other than receiving transfers from Epstein’s personal accounts. The report contains account-level and entity-level figures used to map those flows.

This is one of the most useful parts of EFTA02810827 for GAH because it can be tested against the wider corpus: bank statements, wires, corporate records, tax filings, and later account statements can either corroborate, refine, or contradict the expert’s entity classifications.

7. Why the report matters to the nine-SAR story

The report predates the later Senate nine-SAR synthesis, but it supplies much of the transaction architecture that makes the SAR chronology interpretable. Senate Finance cited Amador when discussing large cash withdrawals, Epstein’s position among JPMorgan’s biggest private-bank clients, client revenue, and payments to Maxwell.

That creates a useful source ladder:

  1. Underlying bank records and transaction data — the base receipts.
  2. Amador expert report — forensic aggregation and expert interpretation.
  3. JPMorgan responses in litigation — disputes about context, knowledge, and inference.
  4. Senate Finance analysis — later government use of the same record family.
  5. SAR chronology — regulatory reporting timing and scale.

8. What JPMorgan disputed

In the civil litigation, JPMorgan disputed multiple inferences drawn from the USVI expert summaries. Among other things, bank responses argued that transaction information compiled by the expert was not necessarily available to JPMorgan in the same aggregated format while Epstein was a client, and disputed the claim that the summarized transactions established bank participation in Epstein’s trafficking venture.

The USVI case settled in 2023 without a trial on these expert opinions and without JPMorgan admitting wrongdoing in the settlement. The expert report therefore remains evidence submitted in contested litigation—not a judicial finding adopting every conclusion.

10. What remains open

  • Transaction-by-transaction validation: every aggregate in the expert report should be reconciled against the underlying statements, wires, checks, and Bates-stamped bank exhibits where available.
  • Entity classification: the “shell company” label is an expert characterization until the specific entity’s corporate and operational record is checked.
  • Purpose of payments: account records can establish amounts, dates, senders, and recipients; purpose frequently requires separate documents.
  • Bank knowledge: an expert can aggregate information retrospectively. What particular JPMorgan employees knew at a particular time is a separate evidentiary question.
  • Redacted conclusion: GAH does not speculate about the report’s redacted final point.
