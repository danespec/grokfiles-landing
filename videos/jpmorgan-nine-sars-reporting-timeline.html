What this video examines

The Short points to a dated reporting sequence: the Senate staff table lists seven reports from 2002 through 2016 and two more in August and September 2019. The reported amounts differ sharply; the filing dates and the underlying activity dates are different fields.

The earlier amounts sum to $4,316,424. The two 2019 amounts sum to $1,282,799,188, with both activity windows beginning October 1, 2003. Because those windows overlap, adding the report amounts does not establish a unique-dollar total.

Senate staff calls for investigation of whether reporting failures were deliberate. The same memorandum quotes JPMorgan’s position that, excepting Jes Staley, its executives acted with integrity and would not have retained Epstein as a client had they known of ongoing crimes. These are competing positions, not a verdict on intent.

The separate 4,725-wire figure belongs to one filing described in Senate material; it should not be interchanged with the two-report sum. The linked full investigation and evidence brief preserve that distinction.

What this does not prove

The two 2019 reports sum to $1,282,799,188 in reported amounts; their activity windows overlap. This is not a deduplicated unique-dollar total, a total of criminal proceeds or proof that each transfer was unlawful. Seven earlier reports sum to $4,316,424. Filing chronology alone does not establish why a particular monitoring or reporting decision was made. Senate staff’s allegations and JPMorgan’s response remain attributed.
