Adversarial method

The manuscript can be useful because it can be wrong.

A hypothesis is valuable only when it produces a testable question. The archive records the answer, including contradiction, silence, and retirement.

Source hierarchy

The manuscript is a search grammar. It can suggest people, places, dates, aliases, timelines, and patterns to test, but it does not corroborate itself. Corroboration requires reviewed public records, archive records, court filings, source images, or other attributable material outside the manuscript.

Claim handling

Each candidate claim starts as written. The reader should preserve the original language, page location, and context before simplifying it. Edits belong in the ledger, not in the raw manuscript.

Bias and silence

Every record has bias: source type, extraction quality, custody, selection effects, OCR limits, missing pages, duplicate records, legal posture, and redaction. Silence is recorded as silence. It is not proof of absence, proof of concealment, or permission to infer hidden facts.

Identity control

Names, aliases, initials, redactions, and inferred identities are kept separate. A candidate entity is not a verified identity. The ledger must show whether the source says a name, an alias, a role, a property, a document identifier, or only a search lead.

Resolution rules

Corroborated, partially supported, contradicted, unresolved, retired, and not-yet-tested are research statuses. They do not imply guilt, innocence, knowledge, relationship, agency, or legal liability unless a cited adjudicative source directly supports that narrow statement.

Publication rules

The landing page and methodology can be indexable because they are original editorial analysis. The raw reader and search interface remain noindex and ad-free. Ledger and entry pages become indexable only when they contain substantial original analysis and attributable archive receipts.

Reader route boundaries

The protected reader is a source-inspection surface, not a search landing page. Its job is to let a reader inspect the manuscript in context while preserving the separation between manuscript language and verified evidence. That is why the reader, search route, raw page APIs, and unfinished ledger routes remain noindex and ad-free. They can be useful to researchers without being promoted as independent conclusions.

Ledger mechanics

A ledger item should begin with the manuscript passage, page location, exact wording, and the reason the passage creates a testable question. The next step is not a summary; it is a source search. The ledger should record whether public court filings, official reports, archive records, source renders, dispatches, or other attributable materials support, contradict, narrow, or retire the question. If the answer is partial, the item stays partial. If the source search fails, the item remains unresolved or retired rather than being rewritten as a finding.

Identity and alias handling

The manuscript may contain names, initials, nicknames, corrupted spellings, or ambiguous references. Those strings are not merged into verified entities unless source evidence supports the merge. A candidate alias can be preserved with confidence, source location, and reason, but the canonical page must explain what the alias is and why it does not create a separate person or organization. This is the same rule used for OCR variants in Banking Records and for candidate-match rows in the death-investigation archive.

Examples of acceptable conclusions

Acceptable conclusions are narrow: "the manuscript contains this phrase on this page," "the phrase led to this public source search," "this outside record supports only this limited fact," or "the available source contradicts the manuscript claim." Unacceptable conclusions skip the source step: "the manuscript proves the allegation," "a name in the manuscript establishes conduct," or "silence in the public record proves concealment." The methodology is designed to preserve useful leads while making failed or unsupported theories visible.

Corrections and retirement

When a reader identifies a bad transcription, broken source link, duplicate alias, or unsupported ledger inference, the correction should point to the exact page, claim, and replacement source. A corrected item may be relabeled, moved back to open review, merged into a canonical entity, or retired. Retirement does not erase the manuscript passage; it stops the archive from asking search engines or readers to treat an unsupported route as editorial evidence.
