Epstein Victims’ Privacy Act Claim Survives DOJ Motion to Dismiss

A federal magistrate judge in California denied the United States’ motion to dismiss five pseudonymous Epstein victims’ Privacy Act claim on November 24. The ruling permits the claim to proceed; it does not establish that the government acted unlawfully or decide the separate claims against Google and X.AI.

What happened

In Jane Doe 1 et al. v. United States of America et al., five plaintiffs proceeding under pseudonyms brought a proposed class action concerning alleged unauthorized disclosure of victims’ private information in the Epstein Files Transparency Act releases beginning in December 2025. The plaintiffs allege roughly 100 victims were exposed and contend that some information continued to be republished by AI services after government withdrawals. Those allegations and the proposed class size are not adjudicated facts.

The sole claim against the United States is under 5 U.S.C. § 552a(b) and (g)(1)(D). The claims against Google LLC and X.AI Corp. arise under separate California statutory and common-law theories. The plaintiffs seek damages and other relief; the court has not awarded relief in this order.

What Judge DeMarchi decided

U.S. Magistrate Judge Virginia K. DeMarchi, sitting in the Northern District of California’s San Jose Division with the parties’ consent under 28 U.S.C. § 636(c), denied the government’s Rule 12(b)(6) motion. The contested issue was whether the plaintiffs plausibly alleged willful or intentional disclosure. The court applies a standard above gross negligence and regards the question as dependent on the facts of individual disclosures.

The United States acknowledged inadvertent victim-information disclosures but argued that Congress imposed an extraordinary 30-day release obligation and that DOJ took remedial measures, including a confidential reporting mechanism. Plaintiffs allege that DOJ prioritized speed and transparency over privacy, failed to act on removal requests, and acted with retaliatory motives. The court held the pleaded allegations sufficient to proceed even in light of the government’s account. It did not determine the truth of those allegations.

The judge declined to use a February 2025 press release, January 4, 2026 DOJ memorandum and January 30 letter to Congress to decide disputed facts at this stage. Even assuming the presumption of regularity applies, the court found dismissal unwarranted on these pleadings. See ECF 81, pages 3–8.

What the order does not decide

  • No finding of a willful Privacy Act violation or award of damages.
  • No merits ruling on Google or X.AI; the claims against them are separate.
  • No determination of the alleged extent of continuing AI republication or the response to any specific takedown request.
  • No ruling invalidating the EFTA release requirement itself. The claim concerns alleged disclosure of protected victim information.
  • No new hearing or discovery deadline set by ECF 81. The proposed class has not been certified by this order.

GAH does not reproduce any victim-identifying information or link to exposed records.

What to watch

The second amended complaint was filed November 13 and did not change allegations against the United States. The court heard the motion November 17. Earlier discovery rulings permit discovery against the government to proceed while discovery against Google and X.AI is stayed. The companies’ motion against the superseded complaint was denied as moot; any further rulings will be tracked separately.
