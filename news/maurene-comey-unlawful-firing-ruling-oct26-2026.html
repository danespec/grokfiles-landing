DOJ accountability · Federal court decision · October 26, 2026

Federal judge rules former Epstein–Maxwell prosecutor Maurene Comey was unlawfully fired

A federal judge in Manhattan ruled on October 26, 2026 that the Department of Justice unlawfully terminated former Assistant U.S. Attorney Maurene Comey in July 2025. The signed, 55-page opinion grants retroactive reinstatement and backpay through December 20, 2025, and declares that the government violated federal statutory and constitutional protections. It does not decide why DOJ targeted Comey or establish interference with the Epstein or Maxwell prosecutions.

Read the signed source: U.S. District Court, Southern District of New York — ECF 96, October 26, 2026 (55-page original PDF).
Case: Comey v. U.S. Department of Justice et al., 1:25-cv-07625-JMF. Judge: Jesse M. Furman. Type: Opinion and Order on cross-motions for judgment on the pleadings under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(c).

What the court actually decided

1. The termination violated the Civil Service Reform Act

On PDF page 3, Judge Furman explains that while 28 U.S.C. § 542(b) authorizes the Attorney General to remove assistant U.S. attorneys, that power is subject to the Civil Service Reform Act's cause and notice requirements. The court found that the July 16, 2025 termination did not satisfy those protections; the government conceded that it was not performance-based or for cause. The judge also rejected the government's argument that Article II gives it unrestricted authority to remove prosecutors despite congressional safeguards.

2. Retroactive reinstatement and backpay were granted—not an order to return to work today

The conclusion on PDF page 54 grants retroactive reinstatement and backpay covering July 16 to December 20, 2025. As PDF pages 48–49 explain, that period can affect pension credits, Thrift Savings Plan contributions, length of service, and the official personnel record. Comey has since accepted private-sector work and is not seeking present-day reinstatement. The court reserved the exact amount of backpay and whether costs and attorney's fees should also be awarded.

3. The court granted declaratory relief but left political-motive questions unresolved

The court declared that the removal violated separation-of-powers principles, the Administrative Procedure Act, and her Fifth Amendment due-process and statutory rights. Comey alleged that she was targeted because of her relationship to former FBI Director James Comey or perceived political views. The ruling does not make an evidentiary finding that either alleged motive was proven. Her First Amendment claim (Count Four) remains unresolved; the judge directed her to explain by November 9, 2026 why it should not be dismissed as potentially moot, with the government permitted a response one week afterward. See PDF page 53.

4. Not every claim succeeded

According to the signed disposition, Comey prevailed on Counts One, Two, Five and Six. The government prevailed on Count Three; Counts Seven, Eight, Nine and Ten were dismissed. Judgment on Count Four was reserved. The court separately stayed discovery in this employment case unless it later orders otherwise. This stay does not govern Phang v. Blanche or the release of Epstein records.

Why this case matters to Epstein-records oversight

Comey was a career prosecutor involved in major federal cases, including the prosecution of Ghislaine Maxwell and work connected to Jeffrey Epstein. The removal of a prosecutor with that background raises a legitimate institutional-independence question: whether personnel decisions could affect continuity of sensitive investigations or public trust in prosecutorial independence. The court’s actual holding, however, concerns statutory removal authority and due process—not evidence suppression, victim-record handling or why Comey was removed. A finding that dismissal was unlawful does not establish a cover-up.

Evidence boundary: The judge did not rule that DOJ fired Comey because of her Epstein/Maxwell work, that the White House directly ordered an unlawful act for that purpose, that prosecutors destroyed records, or that any Epstein-related defendant was protected. Those remain unsupported inferences absent distinct evidence.

Government response and appeals status

A DOJ spokesperson told Axios on October 26 that the department disagrees with the ruling and intends to appeal. An announced intention to appeal is not an appeal filing; this article does not claim that an appeal has already been docketed or that the October 26 order has been stayed. Appellate status and any subsequent modification require a separately verified docket entry.

What remains open

  • Backpay and fees: exact monetary award and attorney-fee eligibility remain for later determination.
  • First Amendment: response to the November 9 show-cause deadline and any subsequent signed disposition.
  • Appeal: a verified notice of appeal, appellate docket, or stay order—not merely a DOJ statement.
  • Epstein link: any primary evidence directly tying the termination motive to Epstein/Maxwell-case decisions, as distinct from Comey's publicly documented work on those prosecutions.
