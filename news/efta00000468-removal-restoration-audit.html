EFTA00000468: What the DOJ Removed, Restored, and What Remains Unverified

A dated audit of the December 2025 disappearance and restoration, the unproved September 2026 claim, and an October 14 access check that reached age verification rather than the PDF.

Grok Archive Hub News Desk · Published October 14, 2026 · Source status checked October 14, 2026 · No source photograph reproduced

Verdict as of October 14: A temporary December 2025 removal followed by a December 21 restoration is documented. The claim that the file remains deleted, or was deleted again on September 28–29, 2026, is not independently verified. Our October 14 automated check did not retrieve the source PDF because DOJ presented age verification to this client. That result is not a missing-file finding.

What the documents and dated reports establish

On December 19, 2025, the Justice Department published its first Epstein-files dataset. It included a one-page document identified as EFTA00000468.pdf, an image of a desk drawer containing other photographs, one of which featured Donald Trump. Its appearance in this evidence photograph does not establish participation in or knowledge of Epstein's criminal activity. We are not reproducing the file's images or attempting to identify any other people depicted.

On December 20, the document ceased to appear in the Department's sequential public listing and could not be retrieved through the previously published link. The fact-checking newsroom Lead Stories captured the missing-listing state between neighboring file numbers and retained evidence of the original release. That is direct contemporary documentation of a temporary public-access change—not merely an inference from a later failed automated request.

On Sunday, December 21, DOJ said that the Southern District of New York had flagged the image for potential victim-protection concerns. According to DOJ's public explanation, its subsequent review found no evidence of Epstein victims in the photograph, and the file was restored without alteration or additional redaction. Reuters reported the restoration that day. The no-alteration assertion is DOJ's account: this GAH audit did not obtain original and restored PDF bytes to perform an independent cryptographic comparison.

Our October 14 DOJ access experiment: a control matters

GAH attempted requests against the DOJ Dataset 1 paths for EFTA00000466.pdf, EFTA00000467.pdf, EFTA00000468.pdf, EFTA00000469.pdf and EFTA00000470.pdf. Every target redirected to /age-verify and ultimately returned an HTML age-verification page rather than a PDF. The same behavior across the target and neighboring controls makes it methodologically unsound to single out EFTA00000468 as deleted on the basis of those requests.

We also queried portions of the DOJ Dataset 1 public listing. Its first page was accessible, while several paginated-listing requests were denied to our client. Because the target's listing segment could not be independently inspected, this does not establish whether the target is listed there at the time of writing. An HTTP 200 response for a redirected age-verification HTML page must not be reported as a successful PDF fetch.

Separately, an independent research index continues to catalog the file as a one-page, approximately 376.2 KB PDF. That is archive-inventory evidence that the source was known and cataloged, not a live October 14 DOJ response or byte-for-byte comparison with any December version. An independent deletion-audit page states that on September 27 it re-requested nine previously flagged URLs and found none still missing. That retrospective sample is not a logged September 28/29 verification of this specific file.

What the restoration does — and does not — answer

Three propositions must be kept distinct. First, the temporary December removal is documented. Second, DOJ offered a victim-protection explanation and announced restoration; no available record in this review independently establishes a contrary political motive. Third, whether the exact file was directly retrievable by an age-verified DOJ visitor on October 14 remains untested in this audit.

It is fair to scrutinize why a public source was temporarily withdrawn and how the government communicated that fact. It is not fair to transform that into an established finding of permanent deletion, cover-up, or criminal conduct by someone visible in the image. Critics questioned DOJ's transparency at the time; DOJ attributed its review to concerns about protecting victims. Both are documented positions, not interchangeable with proof of an undisclosed motive.

What would settle the September claim or today's status?

A firm September 28–29 conclusion requires an independently preserved dated response showing the correct canonical PDF path, request time, response status, content type and redirect chain; ideally, the concurrent official listing and adjacent control URLs. Proof of actual content replacement requires authenticated PDF bytes and contemporaneous SHA-256 hashes or equivalent archive fixity evidence. A DOJ age-verification redirect, crawler denial, mistaken dataset URL, or unsupported social-media screenshot does not meet this threshold.

For a present-day availability verdict, the decisive check is an authorized age-verified browser retrieval of the official PDF, confirmation that the response is genuinely application/pdf, and recording of the file's byte count and SHA-256 without publishing the image. Even that would prove successful availability at that moment, not continuous availability across previous dates.

GAH will update this audit if an authenticated DOJ file retrieval, new official explanation, dated listing diff or properly preserved September access log materially changes the record. Until then, the historical remove-and-restore finding is established; any alleged second deletion is an open lead.
