Trump's Epstein Birthday-Letter Defamation Complaint Dismissed With Prejudice

U.S. District Judge Darrin P. Gayles dismissed President Donald Trump's amended defamation complaint against Dow Jones, The Wall Street Journal's publisher, and other defendants with prejudice on October 31, 2026. The court found the pleading failed to plausibly allege that the defendants published the Epstein birthday-letter article with actual malice. The ruling did not decide whether Trump wrote the disputed letter, or whether the Journal's story was true.

What Judge Gayles actually ruled

1. The amended complaint failed the actual-malice pleading test

As a public-figure plaintiff, Trump had to plead sufficient facts supporting an inference that the publisher knew a statement was false or published while seriously doubting its truth. The court found the amended complaint's assertions of actual malice too conclusory and sometimes inconsistent with the reporting itself. The article had included Trump's denial, and the court held that publishing despite a denial alone does not establish actual malice. See PDF pages 13–17.

2. Dismissal is with prejudice at the district-court pleading stage

The October 31 order grants defendants' motion to dismiss at ECF 68 and dismisses the First Amended Complaint, ECF 67, with prejudice. This means the district court has not granted a third opportunity to rewrite that complaint. It is not a jury verdict, nor does it eliminate the possibility of appellate review. The order is final as to the dismissed pleading, while fee-related proceedings remain possible. See PDF page 20.

3. Attorneys' fees are not yet awarded

The court reserved jurisdiction over attorneys' fees and costs and allowed defendants to file a fee motion within 30 days of the October 31 order, with a choice-of-law analysis. Entitlement and any amount remain undetermined by ECF 80. See PDF page 20.

What the ruling does not prove

The judge explicitly declined to resolve whether the Journal's claims were true or defamatory because the actual-malice pleading issue was sufficient to decide the motion. The court also declined to treat the birthday-book page produced by the Epstein estate as an undisputed match for the letter described by the Journal, given the authenticity dispute. Authorship and authenticity remain contested factual questions, not findings against either side. See PDF page 11 and page 13.

Trump maintains that he did not write or sign the letter. The Journal reported on a letter bearing his name in an album assembled for Epstein's 50th birthday and included his denial. ECF 80 resolves the adequacy of the pleading, not the historical truth of that allegation.

Document-backed chronology

  • July 17, 2025: The Wall Street Journal published its account of an Epstein birthday-album letter attributed to Trump; the article included his denial, according to the court's description.
  • July 18, 2025: Trump filed the original defamation complaint, ECF 1.
  • April 2026: The district court dismissed the original complaint but permitted amendment, as recounted in ECF 80.
  • May 27, 2026: Trump filed the First Amended Complaint, ECF 67.
  • August 5, 2026: The court heard argument on the renewed motion to dismiss.
  • October 31, 2026: Signed ECF 80 grants dismissal with prejudice and reserves the fee issue.

Why this First Amendment ruling matters

The New York Times Co. v. Sullivan actual-malice standard protects reporting about public officials and public figures unless the required level of fault is plausibly alleged and ultimately proved. At the motion-to-dismiss stage, the court evaluates the legal sufficiency of alleged facts rather than conducting a trial into the disputed letter. The distinction is critical: failure to state a viable defamation claim does not judicially authenticate a document, and a disputed document does not by itself show that reporters knowingly published a falsehood.

Open evidentiary and procedural questions

  • Appeal: Whether a notice of appeal is filed and what issues the Eleventh Circuit may address. This report does not presume an appellate docket or outcome.
  • Fees: Whether defendants move for fees and costs, under what choice of law, and whether the district judge awards them.
  • Document provenance: Whether future admissible evidence authenticates the disputed birthday-book page or attributes authorship. ECF 80 itself makes no such finding.
