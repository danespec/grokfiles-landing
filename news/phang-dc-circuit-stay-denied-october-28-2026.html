Appeals Court Denies DOJ Stay of Epstein Foreign-Language Records Review

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit has denied the Justice Department’s request to pause review of foreign-language material under the Epstein Files Transparency Act. Its October 28, 2026, two-page order leaves the appeal alive, refers a motion to dismiss to the merits panel, and does not require immediate public production of disputed records.

GAH published this source audit October 29, 2026. Order dated October 28; appeals 26-5299 and 26-5334, originating in D.D.C. 1:26-cv-01417-EGS.

D.C. Circuit — original signed October 28 order, 26-5299 consolidated with 26-5334 (2-page PDF)

Panel: Chief Judge Sri Srinivasan and Judges Cornelia T. L. Pillard and Florence Y. Pan. The operative provisions appear on page 1; the reasoning continues on page 2. This is a court order—not a party brief or a news article.

Three things the court actually did

1. Denied DOJ’s appellate stay request

On page 1, the panel denies the motion for a stay pending appeal. Applying Nken v. Holder, it says the government failed to establish irreparable harm from starting a review of foreign-language documents. The court distinguishes the work of reviewing records from a hypothetical future requirement to release contested materials.

This is a meaningful procedural defeat for DOJ on its request to suspend review. It is not a comprehensive holding that every withheld or redacted document must be made public immediately.

2. Sent the dismissal motion to the merits panel

Also on page 1, the appellate court refers the motion to dismiss to the panel that will handle the merits of the consolidated appeals. The parties must address the dismissal issues in their briefs, rather than incorporating earlier arguments by reference. That is a procedural instruction, not a denial or grant of dismissal.

3. Left room for later disputes over lawful redactions

On page 2, the panel explains why the possibility of later disclosure does not demonstrate present irreparable harm. If the government finishes an initial review and identifies a substantive legal basis to redact or withhold particular records, the parties may still litigate those objections. The appellate order does not pre-approve any particular redaction or withholding.

Why the review-versus-release distinction matters

Coverage that collapses review into release misstates this ruling. The court addresses an immediate procedural burden: beginning to assess foreign-language materials for production under a federal statute. Its analysis expressly recognizes that publication of disputed documents is not imminent and could require additional litigation. That is why the stay request failed without the court ruling on the substance of every file.

For researchers, the distinction creates a useful evidence test. A future DOJ declaration that review has begun is one kind of receipt. A completed production manifest is another. A decision resolving a specific contested redaction is a third. None should be inferred from this two-page order alone.

Four questions still open

  • Certified administrative-record list: Did DOJ file the certified list with the district court by October 1 under ECF 42, or did a signed order alter that requirement? A partial public docket mirror cannot establish an omitted filing.
  • ECF 51: Did the district judge sign a ruling on the October 1 government motion to postpone the administrative-record deadlines? The motion itself is not a stay, and the appellate order does not expressly resolve that separate question.
  • Record delivery: Was the full administrative record furnished to the plaintiff by October 15? Private delivery may leave no public docket exhibit; lack of a RECAP copy does not establish a violation.
  • Future review and production: What notices, review milestones, specific release decisions or redaction disputes follow the October 28 denial? No production total or completion date is established by this order.

The separate handwritten-notes issue

Judge Emmet Sullivan’s October 9 ECF 57 order directed redacted handwritten FBI notes underlying FD-302 reports EFTA01245620, EFTA02858481 and EFTA02858491 to the court for private in-camera review by noon Eastern on October 16. DOJ’s October 16 ECF 59 notice affirmatively reports delivery to chambers at 10:55 a.m. Eastern. That filing supports the government-reported timing; it does not establish that the judge approved the redactions or ordered public posting. The October 28 appellate order concerns the foreign-language stay and cannot be recast as a fresh ruling on this binder.
