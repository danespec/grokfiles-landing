Before the 2026 Midterms: Epstein Files, Campaign Ads and the Actual Vote Record

The discharge petition, two procedural votes, final passage, and campaign-finance claims tell a more complicated story than either party's attack ads. The documentary record—and its limits—one day before the November 3 election.

By Grok Archive Hub News Desk · Published November 2, 2026 · The regularly scheduled federal general election is November 3, 2026. Based on a research draft dated October 29, updated and fact-checked before publication.

One day before the November 3 general election, Epstein-related messaging has become a prominent campaign theme across House and Senate contests. Attack ads highlight procedural votes, contribution records and associations with convicted sex offender Jeffrey Epstein. Those records are real—but the conclusions drawn from them often reach beyond what they prove.

The strongest audit starts with three document categories: the discharge petition, the congressional roll calls, and traceable campaign donations. None alone is a reliable measure of the scandal's electoral effect.

Read the original records before reading the ads
  • House Clerk: Discharge Petition No. 9, 218 signatures
  • House Clerk: July 15, 2025, Roll Call 194 (211–210), procedural previous-question motion
  • U.S. Senate: September 10, 2025, Roll Call 512 (51–49), tabling the disclosure amendment
  • House Clerk: November 18, 2025, Roll Call 289 (427–1), H.R. 4405 final passage

The petition that forced the disclosure fight onto the floor

Discharge Petition No. 9 was filed September 2, 2025 by Rep. Thomas Massie, reaching 218 signatures November 12 when newly sworn-in Rep. Adelita Grijalva signed as the 218th. The signers were 214 Democrats and four Republicans: Massie, Nancy Mace, Lauren Boebert and Marjorie Taylor Greene.

The petition concerned H.Res. 581, a procedural vehicle referencing H.R. 185, rather than constituting the final recorded vote on standalone H.R. 4405. Its political importance was in creating a route to force the issue despite leadership reluctance. It should not be misrepresented as a separate passage of the Transparency Act.

The absence of other Republican names—such as Mike Collins of Georgia, Jeff Crank, Gabe Evans, Jeff Hurd and Ashley Hinson—from the signature roster is verifiable. But the absence of a signature and a later affirmative vote on final passage describe different actions. The petitioner roster is not a criminal culpability list.

The vote record is more complicated than a campaign slogan

July 15, 2025 — House Roll Call 194, 211–210

The House voted to order the previous question on H.Res. 580, the rule for several unrelated bills. Democrats said that defeating this procedural motion would have let them offer Rep. Ro Khanna's proposed Epstein-records amendment. Ordering the previous question foreclosed that proposed procedural route. It was not a direct recorded vote on the Epstein Files Transparency Act, nor did the Khanna amendment itself receive a recorded vote then. See the official House tally and GovTrack's Roll Call 194 history.

September 10, 2025 — Senate Roll Call 512, 51–49

The Senate agreed to table Sen. Chuck Schumer's amendment No. 3849 directing release of Epstein documents during consideration of the National Defense Authorization Act. Tabling prevented the amendment from proceeding; it was not a direct up-or-down vote on the amendment's text or the standalone Transparency Act. Sens. Josh Hawley and Rand Paul joined Democrats in opposing the tabling motion. Official Senate roll call.

November 18–19, 2025 — final enactment

The House passed H.R. 4405 by 427–1 on November 18. Rep. Clay Higgins cast the sole no vote. The Senate passed the bill on November 19 by unanimous consent: no senator objected, but there was no individual recorded Senate yes vote. President Trump signed the statute November 19, after changing his earlier position. The House roll call and enacted Public Law 119-38 are the documentary anchors.

The complete chronology cuts against oversimplification from either side. Saying someone “voted to cover up” the files, based only on a July procedural or September tabling vote, ignores November support for the final law. Saying only “I voted to release the files” may conceal an earlier tabling vote or lack of petition support. The Ohio FactCheck.org analysis and separate Susan Collins analysis show how ads select the more flattering half of the record.

Do not confuse the candidates: Rep. Mike Collins (Georgia) took part in the House procedural vote; Sen. Susan Collins (Maine) appears in the Senate tabling-vote debate. Likewise, Senate unanimous consent is substantive assent without an individual recorded roll-call yes.

The four petition Republicans: four different political outcomes

Thomas Massie (KY-04). Massie lost the May 19, 2026 Republican primary to Ed Gallrein: 47,539 (45.1%) to 57,822 (54.9%). President Trump endorsed Gallrein. Massie has linked his political break with Trump to the Epstein issue but also criticized outside spending and campaign tactics; reporting cites disagreements about major spending, foreign policy and other votes. The primary loss does not establish why any individual voter chose Gallrein. Massie remains the sitting representative until his current term ends, absent an earlier departure. Certified-result reporting.

Nancy Mace (South Carolina). Mace placed fifth in the June 9 Republican gubernatorial primary, with about 12.1%. Trump endorsed Pamela Evette, not Mace. Mace said her push for Epstein records endangered her endorsement; that is her interpretation, not causal proof that a particular vote or petition signature produced the primary result. She was not thereby removed from her existing House term. South Carolina primary results.

Marjorie Taylor Greene (GA-14). Greene did not lose a primary. She announced her resignation in November 2025 and left the House effective January 5, 2026 after a broader break with Trump that included Epstein, foreign policy and health care. Clayton Fuller won the April 7 special runoff to succeed her, 72,338 to 57,097 over Shawn Harris. Neither event, alone, isolates the cause of her resignation. Georgia special-election results.

Lauren Boebert (CO-04). Boebert faced no opponent in her June 30, 2026 Republican primary despite a late threat of a Trump-backed challenge. She remained the party's nominee. The claim that she “barely won” her 2026 primary is therefore wrong; that description appears to confuse a different election cycle. Her experience further weakens any claim that signing the petition automatically ended a career.

What the donation records show—and do not show

Political donations are receipts, not verdicts. Some donors have documented professional or social connections to Epstein. A legal contribution, photograph, meeting, invitation, or name in a record does not by itself establish criminal participation or explain a later vote.

Ohio: Les Wexner, Jon Husted and Sherrod Brown

The Brown campaign alleged $116,892 in Les Wexner-linked contributions to Husted across 2001–2025. FactCheck.org's review of the supporting records breaks that figure into $3,500 directly to Husted's U.S. Senate campaign, $76,400 for Husted's state campaigns and $36,992 for DeWine campaign or transition vehicles while Husted was on the ticket. A single "$116,000 to Husted's Senate campaign" claim would therefore misstate the scope.

Husted's campaign said it donated $34,300 in remaining Wexner contributions to Freedom a la Cart, a nonprofit supporting trafficking survivors. An August 2019 FBI email identified Wexner in a preliminary potential co-conspirator review, noting limited evidence. He has not been charged in connection with Epstein and denied knowledge or participation in Epstein's crimes, including in a 2026 congressional deposition. Neither a label in an investigative email nor a July 2025 contribution proves that Husted's September vote was influenced by Wexner.

Conversely, Husted's campaign accused Democrat Sherrod Brown of taking roughly $100,000 or more from “Epstein associates.” The campaign's aggregate was not independently itemized in full in the source audit. FactCheck.org identifies smaller, separate contributions from Abigail Wexner, Larry Summers, Reid Hoffman, Casey Wasserman, Brad Karp, Barry Diller and others, with degrees of connection ranging from documented contact to incidental mention in files. Brown took no direct contribution from Les Wexner himself. None of those donation receipts proves Epstein-related wrongdoing. Read the two-sided Ohio analysis.

Reid Hoffman and other Democratic campaigns

Reid Hoffman has acknowledged past meetings with Epstein and regretted them. The draft's traced contributions include $7,000 directly to Jon Ossoff's campaign and $1 million to New Engagement PAC, an Ossoff-linked vehicle; direct campaign and PAC totals are not interchangeable. Additional reported super-PAC sums were not independently reconciled against raw FEC data for this dispatch, so they are not treated as validated precise totals.

Even where a donor's FEC contribution and a committee's attack advertising are each documented, campaign-finance tracing must not assume that particular donor dollars paid for a particular ad. A searchable federal contribution record is a starting point, not a finding about why a campaign ran a spot or why a legislator voted.

Ken Paxton's Texas ads are a separate analogy

The Texas campaign references to an “Epstein-style” plea deal involve the unrelated state prosecution of Adam Hoffman and a criticism of Ken Paxton's office's plea agreement. Adam Hoffman is not donor Reid Hoffman. The comparison invokes Epstein's 2008 Florida agreement as an analogy; it does not allege or demonstrate a Paxton–Epstein criminal connection. This case should not be counted as a record of involvement in Epstein's offenses.

What is not established

Ad spending cannot be quoted as one number, so this dispatch does not rely on one. CNN's October 9 analysis counted more than 160 distinct Epstein-related ads and more than $35 million in airtime, approximately 90% Democratic. Politico/AdImpact's October 26 figure put Democratic Epstein-related ad spending at roughly $36 million, using a later cutoff. Axios/AdImpact's October 21 analysis counted at least $70 million since September 1, but for a broader category mentioning pedophilia, Epstein and/or child sexual abuse: 63 Democratic ads, 52 Republican and three independent. A separately reported $53 million figure for Democratic Senate attacks has not been reconciled to that $36 million subset and cannot be added to it. House Majority PAC district-wide ad purchases are not Epstein-only spending, and Iowa district-level totals conflict among sources. These measures have different populations and dates and should never be presented as a single authoritative total.

Polling does not establish that Epstein is moving votes. Public-opinion research shows concern about transparency, but salient issue rankings sometimes come from advocacy-sponsored or secondary reports. A claimed 3–6-point effect is campaign research, not independent proof. No validated causal study or November 3 election result exists at the time of publication. Assertions of voter “base suppression” are electoral hypotheses, not observed findings.

Future congressional investigation is also not yet decided. House Democrats have said that, if they control the House, they would prioritize further Epstein oversight including financial-institution testimony and possible subpoenas of senior DOJ officials. Those are proposed actions, not issued subpoenas. The October 26 House Judiciary Democrats staff report recommending a special counsel is a partisan staff analysis containing allegations, not a court finding; the White House denies its allegations. A proposed follow-up transparency enforcement measure is not law merely because its sponsors announced it. Read GAH's separate staff-report source audit.

The November 3 election is still ahead. Results could reveal who won, but they cannot alone prove that Epstein advertising caused an outcome. Before voters finish casting ballots, the defensible finding is narrower than the campaigns' slogans: procedural votes and final passage are distinct records; the discharge petition was measurable; fundraising proves who donated to whom, not criminal participation.
