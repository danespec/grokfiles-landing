Epstein Files Transparency Act II Petition Reaches 218 House Signatures

Rep. John Garamendi became the 218th signer of Thomas Massie's discharge petition on November 11. The milestone opens a procedural path toward House consideration of new Epstein-files enforcement legislation. It is not a House vote on the bill, a release order, or enacted law.

1. What changed on November 11?

The House Clerk's official signature record identifies Rep. Adam Smith as signature No. 217 (November 10) and Rep. John Garamendi as No. 218 (November 11). Rep. Thomas Massie filed Petition No. 27 on August 31 to discharge the Rules Committee from consideration of H.Res. 1430. The petition reached the numerical threshold on November 11; it did not arise for the first time that day.

That last signature is the independently verifiable new procedural event. It changes the petition's status from one signature short to meeting the 218-signature threshold. Earlier advocacy for disclosure, the original 2025 transparency law, and accusations of DOJ noncompliance are not themselves new findings.

2. Why the paperwork names H.Res. 1430 and H.R. 185

The petition is explicitly for H.Res. 1430. Its plain title concerns consideration of H.R. 185, but Section 4 of the official resolution provides an amendment in the nature of a substitute. If the House adopts the resolution as written, the substitute would replace the underlying bill text with the proposed Epstein Files Transparency Act II.

That distinction matters: the petition does not directly enact H.R. 9694, automatically amend the 2025 statute, or establish that H.R. 185 already contains Epstein-specific language. The new language is in the proposed procedural resolution and its substitute amendment.

3. What the proposed substitute would change

H.Res. 1430's proposed substitute would amend the original Epstein Files Transparency Act (Public Law 119-38) to create additional means of enforcement, including:

  • Civil actions: State attorneys general, district attorneys, other authorized state officers and victims could bring specified federal suits over alleged unlawful withholding, redaction, delay or concealment of covered records.
  • Survivor access: A victim could request records relating to the victim, with protections for other victims' identifying and medical information.
  • State investigations: Provisions would address state/local prosecutors' access to records otherwise redacted from public view, subject to protective orders and privacy limits.
  • Review of withheld materials: The text would create procedures and remedies for litigating noncompliance; those remedies do not currently arise from merely signing a discharge petition.

These points summarize proposed statutory text, not law currently in force. A litigant's allegation of withholding would still need adjudication; the petition itself finds no violation by DOJ.

5. What this does not prove

Meeting the threshold does not prove that DOJ knowingly withheld any specific record, that an individual named in Epstein files committed wrongdoing, or that a court found violations of the transparency statute. Congressional sponsorship, signatures and political statements are not judicial findings. The status of the separate Phang v. Blanche litigation cannot be inferred from this House petition.

GAH will treat any later discharge motion, House vote, Senate action, signed legislation or judicial enforcement as a separate event with its own first-hand source and date. Survivors' private information is not reproduced.
