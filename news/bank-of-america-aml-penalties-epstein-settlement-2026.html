Bank of America Faces $1.2B AML Penalty After Separate Epstein Settlement

Federal regulators imposed $1.2 billion in anti-money-laundering penalties and an additional sanctions settlement. A separate $72.5 million Epstein-survivor civil settlement was approved in August; no connection between the proceedings has been established.

Grok Archive Hub News Desk · Published October 15, 2026 · 2-minute read

October 13 regulatory action. The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) imposed a $1.2 billion civil penalty on Bank of America over deficiencies in its anti-money-laundering program and suspicious-activity reporting. According to its 95-page consent order, the bank failed to detect or report promptly more than $5 billion in potentially suspicious activity from at least January 2018 through July 2025, including roughly $1 billion of activity implicating high-priority illicit-finance risks.

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) simultaneously assessed $750 million. That assessment is credited against the FinCEN penalty, not added to it: $750 million goes to the OCC and $450 million to FinCEN's payment recipient. Separately, Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control announced an $11,662,930 settlement involving 475 apparent sanctions violations. Together, the distinct announced penalties and settlement amount to approximately $1.212 billion.

The bank admitted the consent order's stated facts and violations, including willfulness under the civil Bank Secrecy Act enforcement standard. That is not a criminal conviction, and suspicious transactions do not by themselves establish underlying criminal conduct by the parties involved.

Separate Epstein-related civil litigation. On August 28, 2026, U.S. District Judge Jed S. Rakoff approved a $72.5 million class settlement in Doe v. Bank of America, N.A., S.D.N.Y. No. 1:25-cv-08520, resolving civil claims brought on behalf of survivors of Jeffrey Epstein's sexual abuse or trafficking. The final judgment dismissed the released claims with prejudice. It did not adjudicate the truth of the plaintiffs' underlying allegations.

Evidence boundary: The FinCEN order does not name Epstein. Neither the October 13 enforcement records nor the August 28 judgment establishes that the two matters involve the same transactions, that the new penalties resulted from Epstein-related activity, or that the bank incurred criminal liability in the Epstein civil case.

Why GAH is reporting this: Both are independently documented episodes of financial accountability involving the same institution. They support a comparison of banking oversight and reporting mechanisms—not a claim of common facts, causation, or a unified enforcement action. The FinCEN order also mandates an independent suspicious-activity lookback and remediation, the results of which are not established by the announcement itself.

  • FinCEN October 13 consent order (95-page PDF) — findings, credits and remediation obligations
  • OCC October 13 assessment announcement — $750 million credited against FinCEN
  • OFAC October 13 sanctions settlement — $11,662,930
  • Doe v. Bank of America, Document 165 — August 28 settlement judgment (court order)
